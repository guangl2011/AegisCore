## 1. Technology Innovation

Commercial outdoor autonomous service robots — turf-care mowers, municipal maintenance machines, inspection platforms — are locked to proprietary OEM controllers: closed boards, opaque safety circuits, and unverified command links. Mixed-brand fleets run segregated maintenance pipelines, and a foreign-manufactured controller cannot be audited by the municipalities that deploy it. Swapping foreign controllers for domestic copies is manufacturing, not science. The open research question is whether one edge architecture can provably enforce safety across heterogeneous, untrusted actuators while translating a single command language to many chassis.

No shipping product answers it. Fleet-orchestration platforms (InOrbit, Formant) monitor and dispatch but never touch low-level control; ROS-Industrial explicitly does not replace OEM controllers; universal controller hardware such as SEER's SRC series targets indoor AMR builders, not safety-supervised outdoor retrofit. A retrofit deterministic safety supervisor with a zero-trust gateway for outdoor robots does not exist today.

The scientific lineage exists and AegisCore unites it: reference/command governors enforce pointwise-in-time state and control constraints; control-barrier-function QP filters compute minimum-norm safe commands in real time; the Safe-ROS architecture separates an intelligent controller from an independent safety system holding its own e-stop authority; safety-MCU patterns enforce fixed-period loops with watchdogs serviced only after a valid cycle. AegisCore contributes four innovations combining this lineage into one verified edge stack:

1. **Deterministic Safety-Bounded Command Governor.** The sole writer of actuator commands: a runtime supervisor enforcing kinetic-energy envelopes, proximity-based e-stop precedence, command-age validation, and watchdog invariants independent of upstream network commands or compromised vendor firmware. Every cycle appends a decision record — command, margins, mode — to the safety log: 100% deterministic safety logging by construction.

2. **Semantic Capability Abstraction Layer (SCAL).** A validated state-action mapping translating task primitives (MoveTo, FollowPath, SetBlade, Dock) into chassis-specific command streams across differential, Ackermann-steer, and hybrid multi-motor kinematics, with per-command latency instrumentation and envelope conformance checked by property-based tests.

3. **Cyber-Resilient Zero-Trust Gateway.** Certificate-based mutual authentication, per-session command leases, and nonce-based replay rejection assert a valid envelope before any command reaches translation; on hostile communication loss or spoofing, a deterministic six-state failover ladder descends to controlled stop and never defaults to nominal.

4. **Domestic Hardware-Assurance Topology.** A modular control-board topology engineered for verifiable U.S. surface-mount assembly, with hardware root of trust and auditable microcode, aligned with NDAA Section 889 and federal covered-list procurement rules.

In simulation the complete stack already runs end to end: a 100-trajectory benchmark across three reference chassis measures translation latency p50 26.7 µs and p99 65.5 µs against the 5 ms aim; an eight-fault-class matrix shows maximum fail-safe transition of 30 ms against the 50 ms aim; kinematic violations are zero; and all 1,998,144 governor decisions are logged.

## 2. Technical Objectives and Challenges

Phase I validates determinism, latency bounds, and cross-platform generalizability of the universal control supervisor across three dissimilar mower chassis (differential-drive electric, Ackermann-steer, hybrid multi-motor). Each aim is a research question with a named experiment. Results from the CI-run bench harness — a fixed-seed 100-trajectory corpus and an 8-fault × 3-chassis fault matrix — are reported as measurements; evidence requiring hardware stays a Phase I target with its experiment named.

**Aim 1 — Semantic translation.** *Research question:* can one semantic abstraction translate task primitives across three kinematic models in under 5 ms per command with zero kinematic violations? *Experiment:* the fixed-seed 100-trajectory benchmark measures per-command translation latency and asserts actuator-limit conformance via property-based tests. *Measured result:* p50 26.7 µs, p99 65.5 µs, maximum 158.2 µs across 1,161 commands on three chassis profiles — two orders of magnitude under the bound — with zero kinematic violations. Phase I reproduces it on live OEM adapters, where risk shifts to proprietary CAN and serial drivers.

**Aim 2 — Deterministic edge safety governor.** *Research question:* does a six-state failover governor guarantee a defined safe state within 50 ms under arbitrary fault injection, with complete decision logging? *Experiment:* a fault matrix crossing eight fault classes — spoofed command, stale heartbeat, sensor loss, network loss, watchdog miss, braking loss, proximity e-stop, unmodeled fault — crossed with three chassis, asserting latency, safe-state termination, and log completeness per scenario. *Measured result:* maximum transition latency 30 ms across all 24 scenarios; every scenario ended in a defined safe state; 1,998,144 of 1,998,144 governor decisions logged (100%); unmodeled faults default to controlled stop, never nominal. These are algorithmic latencies in simulation, not hard real-time guarantees — Python cannot prove WCET. Phase I moves enforcement onto a dedicated safety MCU, where the fixed-period loop and hardware watchdog make the bound a hardware contract, and measures scheduler jitter on target hardware.

**Aim 3 — Adapter complexity and field trials.** *Research question:* does one validated profile per chassis cut adapter configuration effort by at least 30% versus per-OEM integration, with zero safety compromise under loss-of-network? *Experiment:* count the integrator-facing configuration surface for each AegisCore profile against a per-OEM baseline, then measure operators' effort in hardware-in-the-loop bench setups and instrumented outdoor trials on three platforms. Simulation cannot produce this number, so the MVP ships the measurement scaffold — configuration-surface counting and the network-loss fault class — and ≥30% remains a Phase I target measured in trials.

**Key technical challenges.** *Kinematic mismatch:* adapting command authority across machines with differing torque curves and stopping inertias without per-variant tuning — profile-declared limits plus governor envelope projection. *Low-latency determinism:* invariant checks alongside cryptographic verification on low-power microcontrollers without jitter — the safety-MCU partition, with Phase I jitter measurement. *Fault isolation:* trapping faults inside proprietary motor drivers without cascading bus faults — bounded-deceleration fallbacks and the braking-loss e-stop path.

## 3. Market Opportunity

The commercial robotic mower market — robotic turf-care equipment for contractors, golf courses, campuses, and municipal grounds — is projected to grow from $2.1–2.8B in 2025 to about $3.4B by 2030, a 10–14% CAGR (Ken Research 2025; DataIntelo 2025; Mordor Intelligence 2026). Policy converts growth into procurement: in July 2026 the FCC added foreign-produced advanced robotic devices to its Covered List, citing data exfiltration, remote disruption, and possible remote commandeering; an October 2026 White House proclamation flags imported-robotics cyber risk; CISA and FBI guidance requires bill-of-materials transparency, U.S.-controlled updates, and independent testing. Municipalities must now justify foreign-connected equipment; domestic, verifiable, NDAA-compliant edge control is a procurement necessity, not a preference.

The pain is pre-existing: mixed-brand fleets run segregated maintenance pipelines on disparate proprietary controllers; operators report single-source OEM replacement boards with 8–12 week lead times (operator-reported) and liability exposure with no independent incident record.

Beachhead: domestic landscape contractors and autonomous-mower fleet operators running 20–150 units, starting with pilot operators already supplying chassis, field logs, and trial sites. Spotlize1 monetizes three ways: sales of the U.S.-assembled, NDAA-compliant controller/gateway hardware module; recurring per-device annual software licenses for the supervisor and telemetry; and hardware-migration certification packages for service integrators. After Phase I the same controller, profiles, and safety evidence extend to snow removal, airport perimeter inspection, and industrial sweepers.
